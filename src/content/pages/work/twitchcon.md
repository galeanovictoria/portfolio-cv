    labelLevel: h2
    contentSections:
      - _component: building-blocks/core-elements/text
        text: >-
          TwitchCon is the culmination of every Twitch event of the year, seen by millions.
          The brief was narrow — wireframes to guide development — but the real challenge
          underneath it was communication: showing new and returning visitors what they'd
          *actually* experience, beyond knowing their favourite streamers would be there.


          That created a tension. Twitch wanted to sell full weekend passes as well as
          single-day tickets — which meant giving enough detail to justify a multi-day
          commitment, while keeping the site welcoming to someone attending their first
          digital entertainment expo. And the schedule wasn't finalised yet.
        size: lg
        alignmentHorizontal: start

  - _component: page-sections/case-studies/labelled-block

    label: What I did
    labelLevel: h2
    contentSections:
      - _component: building-blocks/core-elements/text
        text: >-
          I designed a flow that earns the ask: hero introduction → what the experience
          actually is → regional detail → tickets. Visitors understand the value before
          they're asked to buy.


          Without a schedule to point at, I made the weekend pass tangible another way — a
          prominent hero component above the fold, and a short animation on the nav ticket
          button revealing what the full pass includes: the parties, the exclusive content.
          Small moves aimed squarely at uncertainty.


          I worked in low-fidelity greyscale deliberately. With 10–12 stakeholders across
          Twitch, greyscale kept every conversation on layout, hierarchy and information
          architecture instead of colour — and gave the development team a clean foundation
          to build against.


          Accessibility was a principle, not a pass at the end: on-screen content —
          headings especially — is real HTML text rather than baked into video, so screen
          readers reach it (and search engines do too). For attendees on-site I designed a
          mobile schedule with filters for day, time and room, plus search, so nobody has to
          hunt for the thing they came for.
